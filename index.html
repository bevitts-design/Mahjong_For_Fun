Introduction to American Mah Jongg

🖼️ Visual Cue: Display the current 2026 Red NMJL card alongside a standard tile set and four racks to show students what a full setup looks like.
  • ▸
    Annual NMJL Card — Winning hands change every year. Players must buy a new card to play. Released every April.
  • ▸
    152 Tiles — That's 8 more than the Chinese standard. The extras are the Jokers.
  • ▸
    8 Jokers — Powerful wild cards unique to this version of the game.
  • ▸
    The Racks — Players use physical racks to hold tiles upright and hidden from opponents.
  • ▸
    The Charleston — A mandatory tile-passing ritual performed before the game begins.
📋 Instructor Notes American Mah Jongg is a game of strategy and transition. Because hands change annually, the game stays fresh and players must adapt every year. This is also a great point to mention that players from Chinese, Japanese, or Hong Kong Mahjong are essentially learning a new game — the structure, winning hands, and Joker mechanics are all unique to the American version.

✅
Jokers CAN substitute in groups of 3 or more identical tiles — Pungs, Kongs, Quints, and Sextets.
❌
Jokers CANNOT be used in Singles, Pairs, NEWS (N+E+W+S), or Year hands (e.g., 2-0-2-6). These are single-tile groups.
🔄
Swap Rule: If an opponent exposes a group containing a Joker, you may swap the real tile for that Joker on your turn and keep it.
📋 Instructor Notes The Joker rules are the #1 source of beginner errors. Drill this repeatedly: you must draw natural tiles for any Single or Pair on the card — Jokers cannot help there. Also introduce the Joker Swap concept here — it's a powerful move students should look for every turn.

Deciphering the NMJL Card

🎨 Visual Cue: Hold up the actual NMJL card. Point to a 1-color hand, a 2-color hand, and a 3-color hand to anchor the color-coding system.

Colors tell you HOW MANY suits — not which ones. You choose.

111 333 555
1 color = all tiles from the same suit
111 333 555
2 colors = two different suits required
111 222 333
3 colors = all three suits used

They stay flexible within a category, watching what opponents discard, and shift hands as tiles become available or unavailable. This is the key habit that separates intermediate players from beginners.

The Ritual: Setup & Dealing

🗺️ Visual Cue: Walk through this live at the table — build the wall, have East roll the dice, and deal one round slowly so students see the counter-clockwise flow.
1

Shuffle all 152 tiles face-down in the center of the table.

2

Build the wall — Each player stacks tiles face-down, two layers high, along the inside of their rack edge.

3

East (Dealer) rolls two dice. Count from the right end of East's wall to find the break point.

4

Deal counter-clockwise — 4 tiles at a time, three full rounds, then 1 more tile each.

5

East takes one extra tile → 14 total. All other players hold 13. East discards first to start the game.

📋 Instructor Notes East is also called Player 1. Good etiquette: don't look at your tiles until the entire deal is finished — this protects against misdeals. Remind students why East starts with 14: because East discards first without drawing, immediately bringing them back to 13. The Charleston follows the deal before any gameplay begins.

The Charleston: Passing Tiles

🔵 Visual Cue: Use a simple table diagram with four seats. Draw arrows showing the direction of each pass — Right, Across, Left — reversing for the second Charleston.

1st Charleston — Mandatory

→ Pass 3 tiles to the RIGHT
↔ Pass 3 tiles ACROSS
← Pass 3 tiles to the LEFT ⭐ Blind option

2nd Charleston — Optional

← Pass 3 tiles to the LEFT
↔ Pass 3 tiles ACROSS
→ Pass 3 tiles to the RIGHT ⭐ Blind option

⭐ The Blind Pass

On the final pass of each Charleston, you may "steal" up to 3 tiles from your incoming pass — without looking at them first. Pass them face-down sight unseen.

Courtesy Pass — Optional

After both Charlestons, a final 1–3 tile swap with the player across from you. Both players must agree. Tiles pass simultaneously.

  • ▸
    All passes are simultaneous — select your 3 tiles face-down first, then everyone passes at once.
  • ▸
    Any player can stop the 2nd Charleston before it begins. One "no" ends it.
  • ▸
    Strategy: Keep Jokers. Pass tiles that don't fit any hand. Aim to have 2–3 possible hands after passing ends.
📋 Instructor Notes The goal of the Charleston is to build your hand exponentially before play begins — it's the most social and strategic part of the game. Stress that all passes are simultaneous: you cannot look at what you receive before sending your own tiles in the same pass. Common beginner mistake — looking first, then passing.

Gameplay: Pick, Discard & Claim

🔁 Visual Cue: Play through 2–3 turns live at the table. Demonstrate a Joker swap by showing an opponent's exposed group and performing the exchange.
1
Pick
Draw 1 tile from the wall. Rack it.
2
Assess
Does it complete your hand? Update your target.
3
Discard
Place 1 tile face-up. Name it aloud.
4
Claim?
Anyone may call it to complete an X group — or win!
When your 14 tiles match one line on the card exactly →
🀄 MAHJONG!
Reveal your full hand. Other players verify against the card.
  • ▸
    Play moves to the right (counter-clockwise). East discards first without drawing.
  • ▸
    Claiming discards — Only to complete an X (Exposed) group. C (Concealed) groups: draw only. Exception: you may always call a discard to win, even on a concealed hand.
  • ▸
    Joker Exchange — Instead of drawing, swap a real tile from your hand for a Joker in an opponent's exposed group. Keep the Joker — it's gold.
  • ▸
    Dead discard — Once the next player touches the wall to draw, the previous discard is gone. Cannot be claimed.
  • ▸
    Jokers cannot be discarded by choice — discard from your hand first. A discarded Joker is lost; no one may claim it.
  • ▸
    Dead Hand — If you expose a wrong group, your hand is dead. You keep discarding but cannot win that round.

📋 Instructor Notes Key reminders: once the next player draws, the last discard is "dead" — no one can call it. You can never claim a discarded Joker. Also reinforce the Joker Swap — this is a move students often forget mid-game. End the session with a slow practice round, narrating each turn aloud. The first full game is always the best teacher.
